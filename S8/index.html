The Chronological Narrative

10 Years of Attention: How AI Kept Trying to Pay the Bill

10 years since Attention Is All You Need. 29 mechanisms. Not one of them free.
Vanilla attention was never wrong — it was expensive. Everything since is a payment plan for that bill, and every plan charges you something: recall, quality, generality, or your ability to extend context later. Sorted by launch date, you can watch the field change its mind four times.

By Stephen Raj Arokiasamy

1 · The short story

Five beats. If you only read one section, read this one — no maths required.

1

Attention is a meeting where everyone must listen to everyone

When a model reads the cat sat on the mat, the word mat needs to know that sat and cat matter and the mostly doesn't. Attention is the machinery that works that out: every word looks at every other word, scores how relevant each one is, and blends them together in those proportions.

Six people in a room, all listening to each other: fine. Ten thousand people: impossible. Not because listening is the wrong idea — because the pairs explode. Six words is 36 comparisons. Ten thousand words is a hundred million.

Why you've felt this: it is the reason a model's "context window" is a number somebody has to decide, and the reason that number was 2,048 tokens in GPT-3 and is now measured in millions. Every increase had to be paid for.
2

There are exactly two bills, and almost every idea since 2017 attacks one of them

The reading bill. Compare-everything-to-everything grows with the square of the length. Double the document, quadruple the work.

The remembering bill. While the model writes its reply, it keeps notes on every word so far so it doesn't have to re-read them. Those notes grow with length — and they belong to your conversation alone. The model's weights are shared across every user on the planet; your notes are not. That is the real reason long chats and big documents cost more.

Why you've felt this: long-context pricing. When DeepSeek shipped V3.2 in late 2025 with a cheaper way to handle long inputs, it cut its API prices by more than half the same day. The next section puts real numbers on both bills — they add up to the largest capex programme in corporate history.
3

The field spent 2019 and 2020 fixing the wrong bill

Six papers in eighteen months attacked the reading bill: read fewer words, read them in clusters, read them through a sliding window, replace the comparison with a cheaper approximation. Exactly one paper in that window attacked the remembering bill — and it was then ignored for three and a half years.

The idea wasn't missing. The pressure was. In 2019 nobody was serving a chatbot to a hundred million people, so nobody's per-user notes were the problem yet.

What changed: ChatGPT, late 2022. The moment these models became a service rather than a paper, the ignored 2019 idea was revived — and it is now in almost everything you use, including the Llama family.
4

Then one paper ended an entire research wave without changing any maths

May 2022. FlashAttention. It computes exactly the same answer as the 2017 design — not an approximation, not a shortcut. It simply noticed that the slow part was never the arithmetic, it was shuttling numbers back and forth between the GPU's big slow memory and its small fast memory. So it reorganised the work to keep more of it in the fast memory.

Overnight, most of the clever approximations from 2019–2020 lost their reason to exist: they had all been benchmarking against a badly-implemented version of the thing they were replacing.

Where it went: straight into the standard toolkit. It is what runs when PyTorch calls attention on an NVIDIA GPU today. The most consequential attention paper after 2017 changed the implementation, not the idea — which is not the shape of story anyone was expecting.
5

Nothing is ever really discarded — and the trend is from deleting to compressing

Recurrence was declared obsolete in 2017, came back in 2020, failed, got a fix in 2021 nobody could train, became trainable in 2024, and is now shipping inside hybrid models. Sparsity follows the same arc: hand-drawn in 2019, abandoned, then trained properly from scratch in 2025. Ideas here aren't rejected. They're parked until the hardware and the training recipes catch up.

And the direction of travel is unmistakable. Early savings worked by throwing information away and paid for it in quality. Recent ones summarise it instead — and largely stop having to make the trade at all.

Where it went: DeepSeek-V2's compressed cache in 2024 was the turning point — it kept everything and squeezed it, rather than dropping heads, and beat the older method on memory and quality at once. That is why models with very long contexts stopped being exotic.

A list sorted A–Z shows you twenty-nine techniques. The same list sorted by date shows you a field arguing with itself, and changing its mind four times.

That's the story. Everything below is the evidence: why this is a ~$725-billion-a-year engineering problem, what attention actually computes, a picture of what the field worried about each year, and all twenty-nine entries with what each one buys, what it costs, and when you'd pick it.

Follow the arithmetic and it stops being a research curiosity. A GPU is, for this purpose, two things you are renting: arithmetic and memory bandwidth. The reading bill spends the arithmetic; the remembering bill spends the bandwidth and the memory itself. Published estimates put the notes for a single million-token conversation at over 100 GB — more than fits on one top-end accelerator, for one user. Meanwhile at 16K tokens attention already accounts for around 61% of the time it takes to produce each token, and by a million tokens it is 96%. Everything else the model does — all those billions of parameters people quote — is a rounding error next to it.

That is the whole reason this timeline exists. Attention is the term in the cost function that scales badly, so it is where a research win converts directly into money. And the conversion rate is brutal in both directions:

  • Down. Halve the cache and you either halve the GPUs serving the same users, or serve twice as many on the ones you already bought. DeepSeek shipped exactly that trade and moved its public price list the same day.
  • Up. Leave it alone and the cost of a long context grows with the square of the length while your users cheerfully paste in entire codebases. Context windows did not grow from 2,048 tokens to millions because someone changed a config value — every order of magnitude had to be bought with a mechanism on this page.

Which reframes the whole list below. These are not twenty-nine competing academic proposals. They are twenty-nine cost-reduction proposals, each one priced by the market, and the ones that got adopted are the ones whose saving was worth more than the accuracy they gave up. That is also why the honest "what it costs" box matters more than the "what it buys" box: at this capex, a mechanism that saves 40% of your memory and quietly loses long-range recall has not saved you anything — it has just moved the bill somewhere you cannot see it.

The most valuable thing on this timeline changed no mathematics at all. FlashAttention just noticed the field had been optimising the wrong resource — counting arithmetic while the GPU was actually starved for bandwidth.

Which is the uncomfortable lesson for anyone spending the $725B: the biggest win of the last decade came from understanding the hardware, not from a cleverer equation. Sources for every figure here are in the repository README.

3 · What attention actually computes

One idea, three names, and a division that looks arbitrary until you know what it prevents. Nothing after this section makes sense without it.

Every word gets turned into three different things, because it plays three roles at once. The easiest way in is to think of a room full of people holding up signs.

QUERY — the question I'm asking mat asks: "who here tells me what I am? what verb happened to me?"
KEY — the sign I'm holding up sat holds up: "I'm a verb, and something did me." the holds up: "I'm grammar, ignore me."
VALUE — what I'd actually hand over The content sat contributes if you decide to listen to it. Separate from its sign on purpose: how findable you are and what you're worth are different things.

Now the whole mechanism, in five steps:

  • 1. Match every question against every sign. Multiply each query by each key. High number = this word is relevant to me. This single step is the entire T² bill — it's every pair, all at once, which is also exactly why it parallelises so beautifully on a GPU.
  • 2. Divide by √dk. The famous "scaled" part, and the one that looks like a magic constant. It isn't. A match score is a sum over dk dimensions, so as you make the model wider, the scores drift bigger purely from having more terms to add up — their spread grows like √dk. Feed huge numbers into the next step and it collapses into "100% that one word, 0% everything else", which kills learning: there's no gradient left to tell you the second-best word was nearly as good. Dividing by √dk cancels the width exactly, so a 64-wide model and a 4096-wide model start out equally undecided. Try it below — drag the slider and watch a soft blend snap into a hard spike.
  • 3. Cross out the future. Set every score pointing forward to minus infinity, so it becomes exactly zero in the next step. This is the trick that lets a model train on a whole sentence in parallel while still only ever predicting the next word from words it has already seen.
  • 4. Softmax each row. Turn the scores into percentages that add up to 100%. This is a competition: for one word to gain attention, another must lose it. Two consequences show up later in the timeline — approximations that drop the competition get faster but lose the ability to say "only this one"; and models under pressure to spend their 100% somewhere often dump it on the first token, which is the "attention sink" phenomenon.
  • 5. Blend the values. Weighted average of what everyone would hand over, using those percentages. That's the output — a version of mat that now contains some cat and some sat.

That's it. Everything on the timeline below either approximates step 1, shrinks what step 5 has to keep around, or fixes the fact that none of these five steps knows what order the words came in.

A third problem hides underneath: none of the five steps involves position at all, so attention genuinely cannot tell dog bites man from man bites dog until you bolt order on from outside. That's why a third of the timeline is about position.

6 · What the order shows that a list cannot

The engineering version of section 1, with the specifics.

  • The field solved the wrong bill first. 2019–2020 is a stampede at T² compute — sparse patterns, LSH, windows, linear kernels — while exactly one paper (MQA, Nov 2019) attacks the KV cache. It is then ignored for three and a half years until GQA revives the same idea in May 2023. The gap is the story: the idea was not missing, the pressure was missing. Nobody was serving these models yet.
  • FlashAttention (May 2022) is the plot twist. It changed nothing about the mathematics and killed most of the preceding wave anyway — by showing the bottleneck was memory traffic, not FLOPs. Approximate methods had been competing against a strawman implementation. In a list this looks like one more entry; in date order you can see the 2020 approximation wave stop.
  • Position gets repaired three times in one year. PI (Jun), NTK-aware (Jun), YaRN (Aug), all 2023, all doing the same thing: patching a model that was already trained. That cluster is the field admitting pretraining is now too expensive to redo — a category that did not exist before 2023, and the reason DroPE later moves the fix back inside training.
  • Then it changes its mind back. Recurrence was declared dead by the Transformer in 2017, returns as linear attention in 2020, fails at recall, gets a working write rule in 2021 that nobody can train, becomes trainable in 2024 (DeltaNet), and gets forgetting in Dec 2024 (Gated DeltaNet). Sparsity follows the identical arc: 2019 hand-drawn → abandoned → 2025 native and trained end-to-end. The field does not discard ideas; it parks them until the hardware and the training recipe catch up.
  • The direction of travel: from dropping things to compressing them. MQA/GQA save memory by discarding heads. MLA (2024) keeps every head and compresses the cache instead — and beats GQA on both axes at once. NSA/DSA do the same to sparsity: not "read fewer tokens" but "summarise, then choose". Deletion is what you do before you understand the structure; compression is what you do after.
  • So what comes next? Extrapolating the oscillation: the open frontier is hybrid layer schedules — not one attention mechanism per model but a per-layer mix of cheap linear state and a few exact-attention layers (V4's DDDGDDDG, Qwen3-Next, Gated DeltaNet hybrids), with the ratio itself becoming the tunable. Expect the next round to be about learning that schedule rather than inventing another single mechanism, plus cross-window state becoming standard, because it is the one bill nobody has really paid yet: every mechanism here still forgets completely at a chunk boundary unless something explicitly carries state across it.
